01Problem

Too little money, and two ways to be right

After reductions in US foreign assistance, including PEPFAR-supported activities, HIV programmes across high-burden African countries were left with gaps. Our scenario assumes one funder commits a fixed US$200M to help close them, split across four interventions: ARV drug procurement, supply chains, workforce training and HIV drug-resistance (HIVDR) surveillance.

The budget should do two things at once: avert as many disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) as possible over five years, and spread that impact fairly between countries. Those goals conflict. Maximising impact concentrates money where each dollar goes furthest; fairness asks for it to be spread out.

And the scenario is structurally underfunded on purpose. The minimum ARV need across the 46 countries adds up to US$872M, more than four times the budget, so every allocation leaves a large shortfall. The real question becomes where to concentrate scarce drug supply.

02Model

184 decisions, two objectives

Each solution is a 46 × 4 matrix of funding amounts: one cell per country and intervention. Impact saturates as spending per person living with HIV rises, so extra dollars help less and less:

DALYc,i(x) = Bc,i · (1 − exp(−κi · x · 10⁶ / Nc))
maximise  Σc Impactc(X)    minimise  stdc( Impactc(X) per 100k PLHIV )
  • Budget: total spend ≤ US$200M.
  • Absorption: each cell is capped by what a country can use, scaled by its PLHIV count.
  • Workforce needs supply chains: workforce spend ≤ 0.5 × (supply spend + US$1M).
  • ARV shortfall: a soft penalty of 1,000 DALYs per US$1M below each country's minimum ARV threshold.

Data came from the UNAIDS 2025 databook (2024 PLHIV counts), IHME Global Burden of Disease 2023 HIV DALYs, and 2026 GHSC-PSM pricing for first-line TLD therapy (US$33.24 per patient per year). With saturation, a bilinear ARV–supply synergy term and the penalty, the full problem is a non-convex nonlinear program.

03System · my part

An NSGA-II that never breaks the budget

Instead of collapsing the two goals into one weighted score, NSGA-II evolves a population of allocations and keeps the ones no other allocation beats on both objectives, producing the whole trade-off frontier in one run. I implemented the pipeline:

  • Seeding: 60 allocations, including the full greedy solution, an impact-ranked greedy variant, a needs-proportional seed and 57 feasible random ones.
  • Sorting: fast non-dominated sorting into Pareto fronts, with crowding distance to keep the front spread out.
  • Arithmetic crossover (p = 0.9) mixing parents country by country, so children inherit whole country allocations without inventing or losing money.
  • Budget-shift mutation (p = 0.25) moving 5–25% of one funded cell to another.
  • Repair: clip to capacity, enforce the workforce rule, rescale or refill to exactly US$200M.
  • Knee-point selection: the frontier solution closest to the ideal corner after normalising both objectives.

For comparison: a greedy heuristic that spends US$1M at a time on the best marginal gain, simulated annealing at five weightings, and a Gurobi LP on a linearised five-country instance as an exact sanity check.

04Results

Most of the impact, a third less inequity

98.6%of greedy's DALYs kept at the knee point
−33%inequity versus greedy (60,159 vs 89,949)
+16.4%hypervolume; converged by about generation 100

Every simulated-annealing solution is dominated by some point on the NSGA-II frontier. The equity-weighted SA run matches the knee's inequity with 53% fewer DALYs. NSGA-II's best-impact endpoint also slightly beats greedy on DALYs alone (4.313M vs 4.299M).

Moving along the frontier from the knee shows why it's the sensible pick: gaining 1.7% more DALYs costs 67% more inequity, while cutting inequity a further 21% costs 6.7% of DALYs.

05What it says

Money follows marginal return, not burden

ARV procurement takes 82–87% of the budget in every method, because the shortfall penalty makes drugs the highest-value dollar while supply is this scarce.

The counter-intuitive part: South Africa has the most people living with HIV (7.8M) and the highest total DALY potential, yet greedy gives it US$0 and Nigeria US$75M. With saturating impact, each dollar covers a much smaller share of a very large population, so South Africa's marginal return is about a third of Nigeria's. The equity objective nudges some money back (US$1.48M at the knee), but only a little.

06Limits

What this model can't tell you

  • The knee doesn't strictly beat greedy. Greedy has more DALYs, the knee less inequity; they are mutually non-dominated. The knee is the better balance, not a free lunch.
  • Near-zero HIVDR funding is a model artefact, not advice. A static one-period model can't see how resistance surveillance protects future treatment; a multi-period model would likely fund it more.
  • Two countries get nothing because of missing data. Equatorial Guinea and São Tomé and Príncipe have suppressed PLHIV figures, so the model effectively covers 44 countries.
  • Point estimates only. Parameters are treated as certain, and the 65/15/15/5 split of burden across interventions is reasoned, not measured.
  • The Gurobi benchmark is linearised. It lands on sparse corner solutions, so it checks patterns on five countries rather than setting a target for the full problem.
ON ETHICSRanking by impact per dollar can underweight absolute need and rights-based access to treatment. The frontier makes that cost visible instead of hiding it inside one weighted score, and the allocations are illustrations, not policy recommendations.
